%% Abstract section.
\abstract{Text-to-image models fill in what a prompt leaves unsaid---medium, framing, colour, background---and they increasingly fill it in the same way. These appearance defaults change with routine decisions such as raising guidance, distilling a model or adding an LLM prompt rewriter, and evaluators judge the change from summary numbers, from descriptions written by vision-language models (VLMs), or from a few images. In a preliminary study of 9,216 images from 12 open configurations, with two LLM rewriters and two VLM describers, we measure appearance along axes defined in words and validated by people, and separate where outputs move from how much they narrow, per prompt. Guidance and distillation mainly narrow appearance; pooled comparisons hide opposite changes across prompts in 38 of 60 cases; a rewriter's strongest effect lies on an axis people reject; and VLMs report differences between two image sets from the same model in 98--100\% of answers, while people reading the same eight-image grids find only 3 of 10 real shifts. Each way of judging fails on its own, and each needs what another provides. We present \sysname{}, a visual analytics system that brings them together: it compares output distributions of a set of configurations at the configuration, subject and image level with live verdicts under adjustable margins and two encoders, shows and checks the human validity of every language-defined measure, and traces claims made in language to the images that decide them. Two case studies and an expert study \pending{pending} show how analysts reach and check findings that summary numbers, generated descriptions and quick looks miss.}

\firstsection{Introduction}

\maketitle

A prompt such as ``a kitchen'' names a subject and nothing else. The model decides the rest---photograph or painting, close-up or wide view, muted or vivid, plain or cluttered---and modern models make nearly the same decision every time: in a pilot, the Vendi diversity score~\cite{vendi} fell from 7.45 for Stable Diffusion 1.5 to 2.79 for FLUX.2 klein, and from 5.95 to 2.32 within one model when classifier-free guidance~\cite{cfg} rose from 1 to 4. We call these unrequested, recurring decisions \emph{appearance defaults}. They change with routine engineering decisions, and the people who make those decisions---model developers, evaluators, and those who select a model for a product---need to know what changed.

Language is now involved in all three steps of this judgement. Appearance is measured along axes defined in words, such as ``muted'' $\leftrightarrow$ ``vivid'' scored by an image--text encoder~\cite{clip}; appearance is changed by LLMs that rewrite prompts before generation, as FLUX.2 does~\cite{flux2}; and differences are explained by VLMs that describe how two models differ~\cite{visdiff,compcon}. Each role produces claims about images, and each can be wrong. A third difficulty is the shape of the data: each configuration produces, for every prompt, a distribution of images, and a change can move the typical image, narrow the distribution around it, or both---differently for different prompts.

We first studied the problem in the text-to-image domain (\cref{sec:study}). On 12 open configurations, two LLM rewriters and two VLM describers, we built an audit method---axes defined in words and kept only after people confirm them, and a per-prompt separation of displacement and concentration under explicit margins---and applied it. The study yields findings about the models, and it shows that each common way of judging, by a summary number, by a generated description or by a look at a few images, fails on its own while needing what another provides. We turn these lessons into design requirements (\cref{sec:requirements}) and build \sysname{}, a visual analytics system in which measurement, language and the images check one another (\cref{sec:system}).

\noindent Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item \textbf{An audit method and a preliminary study} of appearance defaults across 12 open text-to-image configurations, two LLM rewriters and two VLM describers. With human-validated, language-defined aspects and a per-prompt separation of displacement and concentration, it shows that guidance and distillation mainly narrow appearance, that pooled comparisons hide opposite changes, and that language-derived judgements---encoder axes, rewritten prompts, VLM descriptions---are unreliable without image evidence (\cref{sec:study}).
\item \textbf{\sysname{}}, a visual analytics system with three techniques (\cref{sec:system}): multi-level distribution comparison for a set of configurations with live verdicts under adjustable margins and two encoders; validity-aware analysis, which shows the human validation of each language-defined measure in every view and lets analysts validate new measures in situ; and claim-to-evidence verification, which traces statements made by VLMs and rewriters to the images that decide them.
\item \textbf{An evaluation} with two case studies on real data and an expert study against a baseline that offers the same numbers and images without visualization and linking (\cref{sec:cases,sec:experts}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Appearance defaults and diversity in text-to-image models.}
Diversity scores~\cite{vendi,dimcim,grade,attrdiv} quantify how varied a model's outputs are, and studies of default images~\cite{defaultimages} characterize what models produce for empty or minimal prompts. DefaultShift~\cite{defaultshift} compares distilled models with their teachers on a closed attribute list and observes that distillation acts like raised guidance. These works summarize convergence in a number; they do not separate a move of the typical output from a narrowing around it, per prompt, nor lead from the number to the images.

\paragraph{Language in generation and evaluation.}
Image--text encoders~\cite{clip,siglip2} make it possible to score images along attributes stated in words. Prompt rewriters add detail before generation and are reported to add variety in aggregate~\cite{promptexpansion,promptmodifier}. VisDiff~\cite{visdiff} and CompCon~\cite{compcon} describe differences between image sets in natural language, and benchmarks score whether such descriptions name a reference difference~\cite{visdiff} or whether a model identifies a change, with ``no difference'' as an option~\cite{vdiffbench}. None checks the language against a human-validated measurement of the images.

\paragraph{Visual analytics for generative models.}
ViBEx~\cite{vibex} and Varif.ai~\cite{varifai} help users explore how outputs vary for one model or prompt, CUPID~\cite{cupid} visualizes distributions of generated images, and PromptMagician~\cite{promptmagician} supports prompt refinement by exploring the images a prompt produces. LLMs are increasingly used inside analysis workflows, for example to recommend insights~\cite{leva}. \sysname{} addresses a different task: comparing several declared configurations at the level of output distributions per prompt, and verifying claims made about them in language, with analytic provenance~\cite{ragan}.

\paragraph{Visual comparison and uncertainty.}
Gleicher et al.~\cite{gleicher2011,gleicher} distinguish juxtaposition, superposition and explicit encoding of differences, and comparative layouts for many items~\cite{lyi} motivate giving each compared item a stable place. We encode per-prompt differences explicitly, superpose configurations on one map and juxtapose members in fixed slots. Effects are drawn with intervals against explicit margins, following work on how uncertainty displays are read~\cite{hullman}; unlike most effect displays, our margins are interactive and every verdict that depends on them updates.

\subsection{Data}
We use 24 minimal prompts (\emph{subjects}) in six categories (people, animals, objects, interiors, outdoor scenes, activities), each naming only a subject, and 12 open configurations at their model-card settings: SD~1.5, SDXL, SDXL-Turbo, SD~3.5 Large; FLUX.2 dev; FLUX.2 klein-base 4B at guidance 4 and 1, klein 4B, klein-base 9B, klein 9B; and Qwen-Image-2512~\cite{qwenimage} at guidance 4 and 1. Each configuration renders each prompt from the same 32 seeds (9,216 images), and every setting is read from the weights and declared. For the language study, FLUX.2's official rewriter (Mistral-Small-3.2-24B with Black Forest Labs' instruction~\cite{flux2}) and Qwen3.5-4B with the same instruction rewrite each prompt once per seed, and klein 4B and FLUX.2 dev render the rewrites with the original seeds (3,072 images).

\subsection{Method: language-defined aspects, validated by people}
An image's score on an aspect is its CLIP ViT-L/14~\cite{clip} similarity to a right-pole phrase minus that to a left-pole phrase, e.g., ``muted desaturated colors'' $\leftrightarrow$ ``vivid saturated colors''. Because words may not measure what they say, three annotators judged large-gap image pairs and picked the image closer to the right pole, or ``can't tell''; by a rule fixed in advance, an aspect is valid for a set of configurations if at least 75\% of decided judgements agree with the encoder and at most 40\% are ``can't tell''. In round~1, 7 of 15 candidate aspects passed and five were kept: saturation, medium (photograph vs.\ painting), shot (close-up vs.\ wide), tone (modern vs.\ vintage) and background (plain vs.\ busy). ``Style'' ran backwards: CLIP called polished photographs ``stylized'', and people agreed with its direction on only 17\% of pairs. Rounds 2 and 3 showed that validity does not transfer between configuration sets (\cref{tab:validity}). All scores were repeated with SigLIP~2~\cite{siglip2}.

\subsection{Method: displacement and concentration, per subject}
For a contrast A$\rightarrow$B, each subject gets a \emph{shift} $\Delta = (\mathrm{median}_B - \mathrm{median}_A)/s$, where $s$ is a per-aspect scale frozen once from the 12 configurations (one unit is the typical spread of one configuration on one subject), and a \emph{spread ratio} $\mathrm{IQR}_B/\mathrm{IQR}_A$. Practical margins, frozen before any comparison, call a shift of at least 0.5 a displacement and a ratio beyond 1.5 or below 1/1.5 a change in spread; ``same'' requires the 90\% interval to lie inside the margin. A hierarchical bootstrap resamples subjects within categories and images within cells, with seeds paired when A and B share a checkpoint. Aspects, margins, scale and code, and for the language study the rewriters, VLM prompts and scoring rules, were committed before each analysis; primary tests are Holm-corrected. A contrast that changes several settings is a package and is not attributed to one of them, and a pooled result is never read without its per-subject structure.

\subsection{Findings}

\paragraph{Guidance and distillation mainly narrow appearance.}
In klein-base 4B, raising guidance from 1 to 4 narrows four aspects beyond the margin---saturation $\times$0.51 [0.43, 0.61], medium $\times$0.53, tone $\times$0.49, background $\times$0.65---and displaces only saturation, toward vivid ($+0.65$ [$+0.43$, $+0.93]$; 15 of 24 subjects, none the other way). The distilled klein 4B (new checkpoint, 50$\rightarrow$4 steps) narrows all five aspects ($\times$0.41--0.63) and displaces saturation more ($+0.95$), the guidance pattern found by DefaultShift~\cite{defaultshift}, here against a measured guidance intervention in the same family. Under SigLIP~2 the narrowing holds (guidance $\times$0.52--0.62, distillation $\times$0.48--0.58), but guidance now displaces medium ($+1.08$), shot ($-0.86$) and tone ($-1.59$) and not saturation ($+0.44$), and per-subject readings agree only moderately between the encoders (median Spearman 0.33--0.67) (\cref{fig:prestudy}b). Narrowing is robust; which aspects move is encoder-specific.

\paragraph{Pooled comparisons hide opposite changes.}
On validated aspects, 38 of the 60 pooled comparisons that fall inside the margin hide opposite shifts, with at least three subjects beyond $+0.5$ and three beyond $-0.5$. SDXL$\rightarrow$SDXL-Turbo, a package of five settings, is the clearest case: every pooled shift is ``same'' or undecided, yet four of five aspects hide opposite shifts; on saturation, 9 subjects become more vivid and 6 more muted, with ``a horse'' at $+2.33$ and ``a bird on a branch'' at $-2.07$ (\cref{fig:prestudy}a).

\paragraph{Prompt rewriting adds no validated variety, and its strongest effect is a measurement failure.}
On aspects validated for both sides, no spread widens beyond the margin ($\times$1.13--1.49). The strongest result of the 20 pre-registered tests---FLUX.2 dev's backgrounds moving toward ``busy'' ($+0.67$ [$+0.47$, $+0.91$], 17 subjects up, none down)---lies on the axis that people reject on rewritten images (agreement 0.38). Reusing one rewrite for all seeds narrows every aspect ($\times$0.71--0.79), so the variety of per-seed rewriting comes from sampling text. The rewritten text moves toward ``a photograph'' while its images move toward illustration, and per subject the text predicts the image only for saturation (Spearman 0.42--0.53).

\paragraph{VLM descriptions assert differences that are not there.}
Qwen3.5-4B and Mistral-Small-3.2-24B compared 8 vs.\ 8 images per subject for six contrasts and answered constrained questions per aspect (\cref{tab:vlm}). Shown two sets from the \emph{same} configuration (seeds shifted), they reported a shift in 98--100\% of answers, and in free text 90--93\% of their descriptions claimed a difference. The answers were stable across a second draw of images (88--93\%) and not explained by the eight images shown, which agree with the 32-seed truth in 98--100\% of decided cases. They also called narrowing movement (12--13 of 13) and claimed pooled shifts where reversals cancel (49--50 of 50); given a real difference, Mistral-24B read the direction of variety correctly in 79\% of cases. Six new axes proposed by a VLM were understood by people (``can't tell'' 11--25\%) but none passed the check (0.34--0.70).

\paragraph{People with eight images miss real differences.}
Three annotators answered the same questions on 48 of the same grid pairs. On null pairs they reported a difference in 4 of 12 (both VLMs: 12 of 12), but on real shifts they found the direction in 3 of 10 (Mistral-24B: 8 of 10), with moderate agreement (Fleiss' $\kappa$ 0.41 for shift, 0.54 for variety) (\cref{fig:prestudy}c).

\subsection{Lessons}
\label{sec:lessons}
The study answers questions about the models, and it also shows how each way of judging fails: \textbf{L1}~a number can measure the wrong thing (style, background on rewrites); \textbf{L2}~a pooled number hides opposite changes across subjects; \textbf{L3}~one number merges displacement and narrowing, and the displacement depends on the encoder; \textbf{L4}~language---rewritten text, VLM descriptions, proposed axes---asserts what the images do not support; and \textbf{L5}~a few images are too few to decide, while all images are too many to read unguided. Each failure is repaired by what another way of judging provides: numbers by the images, language by a measurement, and eyes by a measurement over all seeds that tells them where to look.

\section{Design Requirements}
\label{sec:requirements}
We discussed the lessons with \pending{n} researchers who train or evaluate image models \pending{confirm, or drop this sentence} and derived five analysis tasks: \textbf{T1}~decide whether a change moves the typical output, narrows it, or both, and under which encoder (L3); \textbf{T2}~decide whether a pooled conclusion holds for every subject and which subjects go against it (L2); \textbf{T3}~decide whether a reading can be trusted---is the aspect valid for these configurations, can the change be attributed to one setting? (L1); \textbf{T4}~verify a claim made in language against the images (L4); and \textbf{T5}~compare a chain or family of configurations at once. They lead to five requirements (\cref{tab:map}):
\textbf{R1}~every verdict is computed from the data in view, under margins and an encoder the analyst can change;
\textbf{R2}~configurations are compared as a set against an explicit reference;
\textbf{R3}~every number leads to its subjects and images, chosen by the measurement over all seeds;
\textbf{R4}~language---axis names, rewrites, statements---is a hypothesis linked to its evidence, and validity is visible and checkable;
\textbf{R5}~findings are stored with the analysis state and can be replayed.

\sysname{} is a single-screen web system built with D3 (\cref{fig:teaser}). \subsection{Visual design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Layout.}
The screen is organized as a drill-down from left to right and from top to bottom, so that the reading order of the page is the order of the analysis (\cref{fig:teaser}). The largest panel, top left, holds the \emph{Configuration Map View}, which answers the first question of every audit---where do the configurations settle, and how tightly?---for all configurations at once; the same panel switches to the \emph{Contrast Overview}, the \emph{Statement View} and the \emph{Audit Trail View}. To its right, the \emph{Subject Matrix View} breaks the selected contrast down by subject; below, the \emph{Image Lane View} and the \emph{Seed View} show the images and seeds of one subject. The right column, the \emph{Effect View}, holds one small scatter per aspect and stays visible whatever the other views show, because every reading elsewhere refers to a contrast and the Effect View is where that contrast is quantified with its uncertainty. We chose one fixed screen over tabs or a dashboard of free windows: analysts move between levels constantly (T2, T3), and fixed positions let them find a view without searching. Each quantity has one home view---effects in the Effect View, images in the Image Lane View, seeds and distributions in the Seed View---and other views restate a number only as a pointer to its home, so that no reading appears twice with two encodings.

\paragraph{Colour.}
Hue is reserved for two jobs. \emph{Roles}: the reference is blue and the focus orange, the only two hues used for configurations; further members are ink and are told apart by their slot and a direct label. A version with one hue per member failed a colour-vision check beyond the third member, and hues for six configurations would have competed with the pole colours. \emph{Poles}: a diverging teal--grey--ochre scale encodes position between an aspect's two poles, with a neutral midpoint, and is used only for that. Status (validated or not, confirmed or contradicted) always carries a symbol---$\oslash$, $\surd$, $\times$---so that it never relies on colour alone.

\paragraph{Typography.}
Three kinds of header element never look alike. A view is named in bold ink (``Effect View''); the \emph{conditions} it is drawn under---the aspect, the subject---sit beside the name as capsules with a hairline border, a muted key and the value in semibold (``aspect \textbf{saturation}''); controls that change the view are square-cornered and quiet. Text inside the plots is two steps smaller and in secondary ink, so that marks dominate and labels are read on demand.

\paragraph{Encodings.}
\Cref{tab:encodings} lists, for each view, what it shows, how, and why we preferred that encoding to the alternatives we built or considered. Three principles run through it. The two quantities every task depends on---typical location and spread---are always mapped to \emph{position}, the most accurately read channel~\cite{cleveland}, and spread is drawn on a log scale so that narrowing and widening by the same factor are symmetric. \emph{Uncertainty and margins} are drawn wherever an effect is drawn: intervals as thin crosses, margins as bands, and a verdict mark only when an interval clears a band. And every mark that summarizes images can be \emph{followed to them}: a dot in the Effect View selects its subject in the Subject Matrix View and its images in the Image Lane View, and a line in the Seed View is one seed.

\paragraph{Interaction.}
Hovering any mark highlights it in every view and shows its values in a tooltip; clicking makes it the selection. A configuration is added to the set by shift-clicking it on the map or from the header, and dragging one configuration onto another compares the two. Brushing subjects in the Effect View selects them everywhere; dragging a band edge changes a margin; the encoder switch, the aspect buttons and the panel modes change what every view shows. Members' diamonds carry their short names, and the Effect View's title explains the marks on hover, so that an analyst can tell which mark belongs to which configuration without a legend panel.

\subsection{Comparison set and verdicts (R1, R2)}
\label{sec:verdicts}
The analyst assembles a \emph{comparison set} of up to six configurations in chain order with two roles: the \emph{reference}, from which every contrast is measured, and the \emph{focus}, which the subject-level views detail. A chip lists the declared settings that differ from the reference and warns when more than one does, so that a change is not attributed to a single setting (T3). Every reading is a \emph{verdict}---moved, narrowed, same, or undecided---computed live from the bootstrap of \cref{sec:study} and the current margins; a change is marked only when its whole interval lies beyond the margin, which is stricter than the confirmatory rule of the study. Dragging a band edge (\cref{fig:design}a) changes a margin for exploration and every verdict in the system follows, which shows which conclusions depend on it; a chip restores the frozen values. The \emph{encoder switch} replaces scores, scale and validity with those of SigLIP~2 and recomputes every view, so a finding that changes under the switch reveals itself as encoder-specific (L3).

\subsection{Multi-level distribution comparison (R2, R3)}
\label{sec:multilevel}
The views form a path from a configuration-level summary to single images, and each level separates location from spread. At the configuration level, the Configuration Map View and the Contrast Overview show where every configuration settles and which designed contrasts move or narrow which aspects. At the subject level, the Subject Matrix View (\cref{fig:design}b) shows each subject's shift from the reference for every member and outlines subjects that move against the pooled direction; a badge steps through them (T2). At the image level, the Image Lane View and the Seed View (\cref{fig:design}c) show the images and seeds behind one subject's numbers. The same panel switches to the Distribution View, which shows the subject's distributions on every aspect, and to the Appearance Map View, a shared t-SNE of image embeddings as an index to defaults the aspects do not name.

\subsection{Validity-aware analysis (R4)}
\label{sec:validity}
Because a language-defined measure may not measure what its words say (L1), validity is a visual property throughout. An aspect not validated for a member under the current encoder is struck through in the aspect selector, its Effect View card and image lanes carry $\oslash$, and its marks are faded; validity is stated per member, so a contrast between a validated and a non-validated configuration is marked as well. Analysts can also define \emph{new aspects in words} (\cref{fig:design}f). The two pole phrases are embedded in the browser with the same CLIP text encoder, bundled and run locally (its ONNX export matches PyTorch at cosine 1.0000; on first use the tool reproduces the stored scores of all 12,288 images within 0.004 scale units), and every image is scored from its stored embedding. The aspect enters every view at once, marked $\oslash$, and counts only after an in-situ check: the analyst judges 12 large-gap pairs drawn from the current set, blind to configuration and score, and the rule of the study decides. Checks can be repeated by the same or other people, each run drawing new pairs; all runs on a set are stored on the server and pooled, as the annotation rounds pooled their annotators, and the result is shown wherever the aspect is used.

\subsection{Claim-to-evidence verification (R4)}
\label{sec:claims}
Statements made in language are treated as hypotheses (L4). The \emph{Statement View} (\cref{fig:design}d) places each statement a VLM made about the reference$\rightarrow$focus pair in the subject $\times$ aspect grid, coded to the aspect and direction it asserts, with the audit's verdict recomputed live from the current data, margins and encoder and the frozen verdict shown when the two differ. The \emph{Audit Trail View} (\cref{fig:design}e) follows one claim---a statement, a rewritten prompt or an axis name---through the measurement that decides it and one subject to its images, including a mark when pooling hides the subject. For rewriting contrasts, the Rewrite View places each rewritten prompt by what its text says about the aspect against where its image lands. A finding recorded from any view is stored with the whole analysis state---set and roles, encoder, aspect, subject, panel modes, margins, brushed subjects, statement---and replays on a click (R5).

\subsection{Case 1: What does distillation do to the defaults?}
\label{sec:case1}
An engineer considers shipping distilled models and wants to know what is lost. With klein-base 4B at guidance 1 as reference, guidance 4 as focus and the distilled klein 4B as a third member (\cref{fig:teaser}), the scatters show both members' diamonds below the spread band on four aspects and the distilled member's further down on all five: distillation narrows appearance like raised guidance, more strongly. Only saturation's diamond lies beyond the shift band, and in the Seed View the seed lines lean toward vivid and converge. Dragging the spread margin shows that the narrowing of medium, tone and saturation survives up to a margin of $\times$1.5--1.6, that of background and shot only to $\times$1.2--1.3. Switching to SigLIP~2 keeps the narrowing but moves medium toward illustration (\cref{fig:case1}a), and the engineer records ``narrows; which aspects move is encoder-specific''. Turning to SDXL$\rightarrow$SDXL-Turbo, the overview shows short arrows but a high reversal count; the pooled saturation diamond sits inside the band with subjects on both sides, the badge steps through seven subjects that turn muted, and the image lanes show Turbo's horses more vivid and its birds more muted (\cref{fig:case1}b). The settings chip lists five differences, so the change is recorded but not attributed to distillation.

\noindent\textbf{Takeaways.} Separating location from spread shows that distillation removes alternatives rather than moving the typical image; interactive margins and the encoder switch show which verdicts are robust; subject-level views and image lanes expose reversals that a pooled number, or eight images of one subject, would hide.

\subsection{Case 2: Can we trust what language says about the images?}
\label{sec:case2}
A researcher evaluates two language components: FLUX.2's prompt rewriter and VLM descriptions of model differences. Comparing FLUX.2 dev with its rewritten-prompt version, the strongest effect---backgrounds toward ``busy''---carries $\oslash$; in the Image Lane View the rewritten picnics scored ``busy'' show the same grassy scenes as the originals (\cref{fig:case2}a), and on validated aspects no ratio leaves the spread band. In the Statement View for SDXL$\rightarrow$Turbo, a Mistral-24B statement about ``a restaurant'' (``more detailed architectural elements such as arched windows and ornate moldings'') is coded to background and marked contradicted; its trail shows the restaurant's backgrounds becoming much plainer ($-1.68$) and less varied ($\times$0.12) while the pooled shift is $+0.11$, and the images confirm it (\cref{fig:design}e). The verdict bar shows that of 290 statements about the pair, 112 are confirmed---mostly about variety---74 cannot be decided and 94 fall outside the measured aspects. Finally, the researcher suspects that guidance and distillation push scenes toward daylight and defines ``a scene in daylight'' $\leftrightarrow$ ``a scene at night''. The numbers fit ($\times$0.52 and $-0.28$ under guidance, $\times$0.50 and $-0.46$ under distillation), but the images nearest ``at night'' are hazy daylight streets (\cref{fig:case2}b), and the in-situ check decides \pending{result of the check}\todo{case}{two people run the night-aspect check in the tool (12 pairs each); the pooled result is read from the server log and replaces this sentence}. For contrast, the axis a VLM proposed in the preliminary study, ``a simple image with little detail'' $\leftrightarrow$ ``a highly detailed, intricate image'', shows a shift toward ``detailed'' in every contrast ($+0.75$ for the rewriter); it is the axis people rejected (0.34), and the $\oslash$ mark and the check keep it from becoming a finding.

\noindent\textbf{Takeaways.} Making validity visible stops the most prominent number when its measure is invalid; linking a statement to its measurement, subject and images turns a plausible sentence into a checkable hypothesis; and a new measure defined in words becomes usable only after an in-situ, repeatable human check.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Implications for evaluating text-to-image models.}
Report displacement and concentration separately: across guidance, distillation and rewriting, the narrowing survived a second encoder while the set of moved aspects did not, and a single diversity number hides both. Never report a pooled change without its subjects. Validate a language-defined measure for the configurations it is applied to---validity did not transfer between configuration sets---and treat rewriter text and VLM descriptions as hypotheses, not evidence.

\paragraph{Implications for visual analytics.}
\emph{Treat validity as a visual channel}: derived measures defined in language are increasingly common in visual analytics, and showing their human validation in every view prevented the most prominent misreading in our data. \emph{Make verdicts interactive objects}: equivalence margins that the analyst can move, with every dependent verdict updating, turn ``is this real?'' into an exploration of robustness. \emph{Language proposes, images decide}: LLM and VLM outputs are useful as hypotheses and summaries, and a system should give each one a path to the evidence that decides it.

\paragraph{Generality and limitations.}
The approach needs configurations rendered on shared prompts and seeds, a measure definable in words, a human check for it, and per-unit effects with uncertainty; it carries over to video or text-to-3D defaults and to comparing LLM outputs along language-defined attributes. CLIP and SigLIP aspects are approximations, and validity under SigLIP is judged on pairs sampled by CLIP gaps. The margins are practical, not perceptual, units, and native resolutions differ (512--1328\,px). The language study uses one rewriter instruction and two open VLMs, and the human grid check has three annotators. The comparison set holds six members, and the expert study is small and formative.

\section{Conclusion}
Text-to-image defaults change with every routine decision, and judging the change by a summary number, a generated description or a quick look each fails on its own. Our preliminary study shows how, and \sysname{} brings the three together: it compares distributions of a set of configurations at the configuration, subject and image level with live verdicts under adjustable margins and encoders, shows and checks the validity of every language-defined measure, and traces every claim made in language to the images that decide it.
